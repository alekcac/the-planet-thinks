The Planet Thinks

Every few seconds, somewhere on Earth, a Wikipedia article about a place is edited. Each pulse is one such edit, shown where that place is on the globe.

  • Colour = language. Each of Wikipedia's ~300 language editions has its own hue.
  • Signed-in — an edit by a registered account.
  • Anonymous — a logged-out editor.
  • Bot — an automated edit, often maintenance or mass updates.

Only edits to articles that have map coordinates appear — about a fifth of Wikipedia. Articles about people, ideas, or events have no location, so they're counted in the header but not shown on the globe.

How it works

The Planet Thinks listens to the public Wikimedia EventStreams feed — the same firehose of recent changes that powers Wikipedia's own tooling. Every incoming edit to an article about a place is matched to that place's map coordinates and drawn on a realistic day/night globe within seconds of the edit being saved. Nothing is staged or replayed: if a spark lights up over Nairobi, someone just changed a Wikipedia article about Nairobi.

Frequently asked questions

How often is Wikipedia edited? Across all its ~300 language editions, Wikipedia typically receives a few hundred edits every minute — on the order of half a million edits a day. The live counter at the top of the page shows the rate right now, and the live statistics page breaks it down by language.

Who is editing? A mix of signed-in volunteers, anonymous readers, and maintenance bots. The colour and legend distinguish them; clicking any spark shows the article and a link to the actual edit.

Why don't I see every edit? Most Wikipedia articles — people, concepts, events — have no location, so they can't be placed on a map. They are included in the counter but not drawn.

Is this free? Yes — free, no sign-up, and open source. Globe imagery is NASA's Blue Marble and Black Marble, public domain.
